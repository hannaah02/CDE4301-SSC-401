Thermal Transmission

Recap

The aim of this subsection is to efficiently transfer heat from the receiver to the TES with minimal power consumption.

Summary of work done in interim is as follows:

  1. Heat Transfer Fluid (HTF) were compared and water was chosen
  2. Method of thermal transmission was assessed and pump was proven to be able to transfer heat
  3. Heat loss was identified and insulative materials were compared with PUR/PIR being the optimal choice.

In this semester, the installation of pumps and implementation of insulation was required in the next semester.



Insulation

The PUR/PIR insulation selected in the interim is compared with the commonly used airconditioning insulation (material: polypropylene random polymer (PPR)), and translucent insulation material (TIM) style insulation (explained later). The choice of insulations and its implementation are explained below.

PUR/PIR insulation is selected in the interim, comparing the cost, thickness required and durability. For this experiment, spray PUR/PIR foam is used with an aluminium duct (7mm diameter) to encapsulate the insulation.

Airconditioning (aircon) insulation is a common insulation that can be bought off the shelves, reducing manufacturing time. For this experiment, PPR of thickness 5mm and inner diameter 17mm is used.

TIM prevents heat loss, while allowing irradiance to heat the absorber (pipe with water). To enable this, the insulation is transparent, allowing transmission of irradiance. It also has a specific configuration between layers made to prevent convection of air between layers by forming pockets of stagnant air (see figure below), which loses less heat. Using the concept of TIM, (single layer) translucent corrugated plastic board is used to wrap around the absorber (hose) to improve irradiance transmission and reduce convection of air. This insulation is termed as 'translucent insulation'.

Insulation Experiment

The details and setup of the insulation experiment are below.

The experiment was repeated to verify results. The results of the experiments are below with extra results in Appendix XX.

In shade

The PUR/PIR and aircon insulation performed best at the start with the same low rate of heat loss, but as temperature drops, the aircon insulation graph has a higher rate of heat loss than PUR/PIR insulation (see below). The translucent insulation did not perform as well as the PUR/PIR and aircon insulation at high temperatures (around 60ºC), but as the temperature fell, the rate of heat loss improved, reaching lower heat loss than both PUR/PIR and aircon insulation (see below).

Overall, the CSP system is expected to function at high temperatures above 40ºC up to 60ºC. Thus, the trend at high temperatures is most important, of which PUR/PIR insulation performed best, with aircon insulation being second best.

Under sunlight

When conducting the experiments under sunlight, 2 different results were obtained where PUR/PIR and aircon insulation performed best in each of them. This is determined to be the result of testing under different conditions: direct and indirect sunlight. When conducted under mainly direct sunlight, the aircon insulation performed better with a lower rate of heat loss, whereas in mainly indirect sunlight, the PUR/PIR insulation performed better. This is theorised to be a result of the different absorptivity between the aircon insulation and the PUR/PIR insulation. As the aircon insulation is of black colour, it has a high absorptivity of about 0.9 [2], whereas the aluminium foil covering the PUR/PIR insulation has an absorptivity of 0.15. [3] Between the two insulations, PUR/PIR insulation will thus have lower energy absorbed than the aircon insulation. This is verified as the PUR/PIR has more stable temperature under sunlight compared to the aircon insulation (see below). This accounts for the variations between the aircon and PUR/PIR insulation results conducted under sunlight.

Under direct sunlight, there is higher irradiance compared to indirect. So, the aircon insulation is able to absorb larger amount of solar energy, which offsets the heat loss causing the lower net rate of heat loss oberved under that condition. As such, aircon insulation performing better under direct sunlight.

From sunlight to shade

In the experiment conducted testing from sunlight to shade, the same trend as the under sunlight was observed, where in direct sunlight, the aircon insulation performed best and in indirect sunlight, the PUR/PIR insulation performed best.

Conclusion

The selection of the optimal insulation depends on the context of the applications. On the rig, the insulation will be largely underneath the reflectors (see below), which will block direct sunlight and will be largely shaded. Furthermore, the application is in Tanjong Balai, which receives mainly indirect sunlight. As such, for the section of pipe above the reflectors, the result from the indirect sunlight will matter more. Overall, for both cases, the PUR/PIR insulation is the best option considering the results above.

Conclusion and Further Work

The integrated system is able to concentrate and store thermal energy, meeting our requirements of keeping water hot at XXºC for XX hours.

From implementation in integrated tests, the choice of insulation has to change. Although PUR/PIR will perform best under the expected operating conditions, it is difficult to manufacture it and ensure that the insulation is done fully and properly, particularly if the pipe length is long. Furthermore, it is very inflexible and thick, making it difficult to implement along small narrow sections. Hence, future work proposed an alteration in integration to aircon insulation. It is preferred as aircon insulation is more flexible, less bulky, and importantly, its performance is comparable to PUR/PIR insulation especially in the shade.

Also, future improvements would include the testing of thermosyphons in the prototype CSP rig, which will be explained in the next section.



Testing methods of thermal transmission

In the interim, pumps have been proven to allow thermal transmission. In this section, thermosyphons in a small-scale system is tested, to assess the feasibility of its application in the prototype rig, assuming that the TES can be placed outside the 1x1m rig size.

First test of standard thermosyphon

To attain a baseline of what the system will look like with flowing water, a standard thermosyphon system is created where the outlet from the tank is at the base of the tank and the inlet is at the top. A copper tube is used and the heat source is placed just under the vertical section where the flow is expected to head upwards back to the tank. To assess if water is flowing thorugh the tube, a water insoluble ink is slowly dropped at the inlet and outlet locations.

The video above shows the baseline, where there is clearly convection patterns caused by flow exiting the inlet tube into the tank.

Second test to observe if height difference of 0m affects the flow

The test is done in the same way as the first, except with the copper tube completely horizontal. As such, the inlet tube, outlet tube and heated segment is on the same horizontal plane.

From the video, there is no convection patterns, and no clear flow of fluid can be observed as in a standard thermosyphon. This is expected as thermosyphone heavily rely on gravity and density differences to drive fluid flow. Regardless, heat transfer will still be observed for example though local convection and conduction, but it will not be through circulation as in the first test, which will improve heat transfer by natural convection.

Third test to observe if the location and configuration of inlet and outlet tube matters

In this section, the small-scale test is based off of the integrated system, where the inlet and outlet pipes are fed into the TES tank from the top, and the heated section is coil shaped. For the test, a small copper coil is used as the heated section, with a tube attached to the bottom inlet that is fed thorough the top of the tank and secured to the bottom of the tank. The other side of the copper coil also has a tube attached that is fed thorough the top of the tank and secured to the top of the tank. The entire tube and coil is filled with water. The coil is heated and the flow tests is done the same way.

It is observed that flow occurs following the manner of test 1, where the water flows up the coil. It is noted that there is a longer time for the flow to occur due to differences in distances between the inlet and outlet. Hence, it is shown that the thermosyphon system should be able to function as an effective passive heat transmission method so long as:

  1. there is a height difference between the TES tank and the receiver, where the receiver is below the TES tank
  2. the TES outlet is located at the base of the tank and the TES inlet at the top of the tank
  3. the entire pipe is completely filled with water (note that it is not a self-priming system)

Future work

In future improvements, thermosyphons would be tested with the prototype rig, to ensure feasibility on the large scale. When implementing, the heat loss through the receiver when sun is down needs to be considered, as observed in the integrated test. For this passive system to work effectively with the TES tank, there needs to be valves at the inlet and outlet pipe that isolates the water in the TES from the receiver. Furthermore, the implementation of the thermosyphon would require the TES to be located outside of the 1x1m rig, taking up more space, which needs to be considered if it is to be used in a final system.



Optimal size of system

In the final design, the system will be connected to the water mains for steady supply of water and pressure driving the flow from the TES to the shower. Furthermore, a system of rigs will be utilised, where multiple rigs will be connected to 1 TES. For each system size (number of rigs in a system), the layout below is chosen to minimise distance between each rig to the TES and allows for modular expansion.

As the system will be pressurised due to the connections to the water mains, the existing fixtures and pipes needs to be altered to fit the new conditions.

  1. The rubber hose will be switched to 304 stainless steel pipe, as it is used in water mains connections, is durable outdoors, and can withstand high temperatures [4][5]
  2. Clamps needs to be changed to relevant fittings
  3. Pressure relief valve added for safety
  4. Non-return valve to prevent backflow

These will be further explained with system layouts under cost analysis.

Furthermore, considering the system of rigs, two connection configurations are considered:

  1. Parallel configuration, where each rig are connected independantly to the TES
  2. Series configuration, where fluid flows sequentially through multiple rigs

We also need to consider the system head, which is affected by the pipe size. Hence, these 3 pipe sizes are considered for the pipes:

  1. 1/8" nominal pipe, smallest common size [6]
  2. 3/4" nominal pipe, commonly used in residential plumbing [7]
  3. 1" nominal pipe, commonly used in residential plumbing [7]

Finally, the optimal system size for the system of rigs has to be determined.

Pipe size

To determine the optimal pipe size, the system head required is compared for the different pipe size. The larger the system head, the more resistance exists in the system that the pump has to overcome, leading to a more powerful pump required. Thus, system head for each system size is used to compare the pipe sizes, considering both parallel and series configuration.

Between the 1/8" and 3/4", there is a large difference in system head in both parallel and series configuration. The extremely large head for 1/8" pipe, especially in the series configuration, with head 1167.41m for 10 rigs, makes it unsuitable for the system. Between 3/4" and 1", 1" have lower system head overall but the difference is minimal (see below). However, with increased pipe size, the heat loss increases. Considering aerogel insulation for 304 stainless steel pipe with commonly used sizing for 3/4" and 1", to reduce limit heat loss to 7.1150W along 3m (5% loss of 142.3W), the thickness of insulation needs to change from 26.975154mm (3/4") to 33.814867mm (1"). The total thickness needs to be minimal, with maximum diameter of 7cm based on experience with our prototype rig. Hence, 3/4" pipe is selected.

Parallel vs series configuration

To decide the optimal configuration, the thermal efficiency of the pipes within the system of rigs will be considered. The length of the pipe is determined from:

  1. Layout of rigs (see figure XX and XX above)
  2. Pipe length estimation (to run loop in MATLAB, see Appendix XX)

Aircon insulation is used as the insulation, based on tests conducted and implementation (see sections above). Using the thickness of 25mm (thickest option commonly available [8]) and using highest possible temperature difference expected of 35ºC, is expected to lose heat at a rate of 7.7145W/m.

Based on these, the thermal efficiency for each system size (no of rigs) for both parallel and series system configuration is plotted.

Based on the results, the parallel configuration is less efficient with increased system size and the series configuration shows the opposite trend. This is caused by the reduction in the average length of pipe per rig with increasing system size in the series configuration, allowing the average heat loss per rig to fall, in comparison to the parallel configuration where the average length of pipe per rig increases with increasing system size. Hence, series configuration is the optimal choice with less heat loss.
