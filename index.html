1. Background

Concentrated solar power (CSP) is a system that collects thermal energy. It redirects and concentrates sunlight using mirrors onto a receiver. The receiver conducts the thermal energy to the heat transfer fluid (HTF) within it, which is then transferred to a thermal energy storage (TES) system or an electricity generator.

Other solar capture methods, like photovoltaic (PV) systems are more mature. However, they are overall more costly, considering maintenance and replacement costs. Furthermore, the lifespan of CSP systems are higher than PV systems.

CSP systems are used globally, with 7360MW in operation and a further 3351MW in construction as of March 2025. Its market is worth USD 5.8 billion (2025), with a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 8.3% from 2025 to 2035.

However most of these systems are large scale, about 5 - 10 acres of land per MW are used, mainly for electricity generation, with an electric efficiency up to 16%.

Despite that, there are small-scaled plants used for industrial process heat and in rural or off-grid areas for electricity or poly-generation. The electric efficiency is comparable with large-scale systems, of up to 15% or 30% for low temperature ( < 500 ° C ) and high temperature ( > 500 ° C ) systems respectively.

For this project, we focus on a unit measurement ( 1 m 2 ) rig for the prototype to develop a modular and scalable solution. Each rig is self-contained but also compatible with one another considering the application purposes. Implementing this system would include having multiple of these modular rigs side-by-side, making it flexible in terms of size and layout.

In terms of application, our industry partner, ES&T Environmental, has identified a growing demand among hotels in coastal Indonesia for efficient and sustainable hot water production. Due to the large space in the hotel and availability of sunlight, a small-scale CSP system is suitable for the location for hot water production. (see Fig X)

ES&T Environmental has also identified inefficient salt production in northern Vietnam due to the poor humidity control in the process. Again, it is suitable for small-scale CSP system due to the space and sunlight availability for desalination to produce concentrated brine. The concentrated brine can be utilised to reduce the production time for salt production.

As desalination also outputs freshwater, its usage can also apply to the hotels in coastal Indonesia due to its proximity to the sea. Furthermore, freshwater as a resource is valuable worldwide as less than 1% of freshwater is accessible for human use in the world. Furthermore, around 35% of the global population faces water scarcity, much of it in coastal regions. Hence, the small-scale CSP desalination application holds value outside the identified case.

2.2 Existing Methods

A CSP system focussing on thermal energy storage consists of:

  • Reflectors: concentrates light onto receiver
  • Receivers: transfers the thermal energy to the HTM flowing through it
  • Heat transfer materials (HTM): transfers the energy from the receiver to the thermal energy storage
  • Thermal energy storage (TES): stores energy

2.2.1 Existing Methods: Concentrated Solar Power

CSP generates energy by concentrating solar irradiance onto a receiver. From this concept, there are 4 main methods for concentrating the solar irradiance:

  • Parabolic Trough: Uses long curved mirrors to focus sunlight onto a receiver tube running along the focal line, heating fluid to generate steam and electricity.
  • Fresnel System: Uses flat, narrow mirror strips to focus sunlight onto a fixed receiver, offering a cheaper, simpler alternative to trough systems.
  • Central Tower (Solar Power Tower): Uses a large field of heliostats (mirrors) to concentrate sunlight onto a central receiver at the top of a tower, producing very high temperatures.
  • Parabolic Dish: Uses a dish-shaped mirror to concentrate sunlight onto a single point receiver, achieving high efficiency for small-scale power generation.

Some CSP system uses solar tracking:

  • Single Axis Tracking system: Single-axis tracking can be either horizontal to the ground, vertical to the ground, or on a tilted axis pointing to the polar star/parallel to earth’s polar axis. Its tilt in all other axis are fixed to maximise the reflected solar radiation.
  • Dual Axis Tracking system: Dual-axis tracking uses either solar azimuth and solar altitude axes or solar altitude and east-west axes.

2.2.3 Existing Methods: Receiver

Materials

Materials chosen to construct receivers should ideally be:

  • Highly thermally absorptive and conductive
  • Able to handle high heat flux
  • Weather and corrosion resistant
  • Durable
  • Cost effective

Most commonly used receiver materials for constructing external receivers include Haynes 230, alloy 316H, Inconel 625,740H and 800H. Among these 740H shows the best lifetime and cost/power performance. However, these metals are less accessible and costlier compared to off the shelf metals such as copper.

Furthermore, high performance receiver surfaces are coated with a high absorption and low emissivity coating. This reduces heat-lost caused by infrared radiant emission from the collector to ambient.

Several studies have investigated the use of copper as a potential material for CSP receivers. Other potential low-cost, readily available, and thermally efficient materials include:

Design

External Tubular Receiver

External tubular receivers are made of vertical tubes arranged in a cylindrical or cubic shape (see figure x), with insulation on the inside. It absorbs thermal energy by solar radiance concentrating on the outer surface of the receiver. They are commonly used due to its simple fabrication, and can be seen in commercial plants like Gemasolar in Spain and the Crescent Dunes in USA.

However, as the solar radiance is concentrated on the outside of the receiver, it results in several issues:

  • Loss of reflected radiance to the environment
  • High loss by thermal emission

Cavity Receiver

Cavity receivers are hollow structures insulated on the outside which receive concentrated solar radiance from the inside. Compared to external receivers, they have the benefit of having:

  • Lower losses by radiation and convection
  • Reabsorption of reflected energy

The optimal cavity receiver for tower CSPs is determined by Bellos et al to be a cylindrical-conical shape (see Fig X) which maximises the thermal, optical and exergy efficiency as compared to other designs.

Bladed Receiver

Bladed receivers are external receivers similar to the cylindrical receivers but with blades instead of having an insulated internal volume. As compared to flatter designs (non-bladed designs), it is better due to:

  • Reabsorption of reflected energy
  • View factor reduction to the environment
  • Both sides are irradiated

Its design is a balance of size and tilt angle. Considering the design, larger surface area improves its light capturing ability, yet increases thermal losses. Despite that, it overall yields a 2% improvement over flat designs.

The optimal bladed receiver design recently published is a 3-star design with overall improvements in efficiency compared to the cylindrical design.

Volumetric Receiver

Volumetric receivers are solar receivers with a porous absorber that lets sunlight penetrate into its volume, heating a heat transfer fluid (HTF) that flows through it. This design increases the heat transfer area, allows higher radiation flux, improves efficiency, reduces heat loss, and is well-suited for high-temperature applications like Rankine or Brayton cycles.

2.2.4 Existing Methods: Heat Transfer Medium/Heat Transfer Fluid

Heat transfer medium (HTM) are generally used to transfer the thermal energy from the receiver to another subsystem for storage, and are liquid, gaseous or solid, with some cases using HTM also as a thermal energy storage. Its effectiveness in transferring energy is determined by these factors:

  • Heat transfer coefficient HTC = k ρ C p t
    • : informs the overall efficiency of heat transfer of the material
    • Specific heat capacity: the amount of heat stored in the material per unit rise in temperature
    • Thermal conductivity: how efficiently heat can move within the material
    • Density: amount of mass per unit volume
  • Viscosity: the fluids resistance to flow
  • Thermal stability: the ability to resist decomposition with temperature rise

The system’s operating temperature and storage requirements are important considerations when selecting a HTM as different HTM have different advantages and disadvantages when it comes to their operating range (because of melting point, boiling point and thermal stability) and energy density (because of specific heat capacity).

Among HTMs, synthetic oils and molten salts are the most common, with new HTMs being developed to improve the performance.

The main categories of HTMs are:

  1. Molten salt, eg. sodium nitrate (NaNO3), potassium nitrate (KNO3)
  2. Liquid metal
  3. Thermal oils, eg. Dowtherm A®, Therminol D-12® y Therminol VP-1®
  4. Gaseous type, eg. CO2
  5. Nanofluids, nanoparticles combined into a base fluid
  6. Silicon-based fluids, eg. HELISOL® 5A

Among them includes emerging technologies: nanofluids and silicon-based fluids.

For nanofluids, by suspending small quantities of nanoparticles, eg. metals, in a base fluid, such as water, it aims to improve the thermal properties, primarily thermal conductivity, which enhances the heat transfer. In terms of nanofluids application as HTF, the limitations are largely due to the limited knowledge of the mechanism, but also caused by its complexity and cost.

Silicon-based fluids are made of linear or cyclic siloxane polymers (siloxane chains with side groups, usually -CH3) that are thermally inert and are highly compatible with standard materials in CSP plants. Its chemical composition and bonds are utilised to improve its thermal properties, such as its stronger covalent bond contributing to its thermal stability.

2.2.5 Existing Methods: Fluid Flow

In CSP systems, the method for moving the HTF is generally through heavy-duty pumps. The choice of pumps depends on the system requirements, such as material properties to avoid corrosion and material incompatibility, operating temperature, total dynamic head, differential pressure, net positive suction head available and flow rate. Pump companies like Ruhrpumpen and Sulzer provide pumping solutions for the CSP system.

Another method is the thermosiphon, which utilises gravity and the inverse relationship between temperature and density which results in natural circulation to drive the fluid. (see Fig X)

It is typically used in solar water heaters as a method for heating water without electricity. In CSP, it is used in small-scale or prototype systems to circulate the HTF between the receiver and the TES, largely in linear CSP systems. (see fig X)

Two-phase loop thermosiphons are also used in some CSP systems. The flow in two-phase loop thermosiphons works by the evaporating and condensing at the bottom and top of the loop respectively. (see figure X) As the two-phase loop thermosiphons require vaporisation, the HTF must operate near its boiling point.

Its application is currently limited to small-scale or prototype CSPs, particularly linear CSP systems as seen below.

2.2.6 Existing Methods: Thermal Energy Storage

Thermal energy storage (TES) plays a central role in commercial concentrated solar power (CSP) by enabling power generation beyond periods of direct sunlight. Nowadays, thermal energy storage is widely integrated into concentrated solar power (CSP) projects, approximately 58% of parabolic trough installations (with 62% of these offering more than 7 hours of storage), 48% of linear Fresnel projects (62% with storage exceeding 7 hours), and 68% of power tower systems (43% with storage beyond 7 hours) are equipped with storage capabilities, thereby enabling prolonged energy generation. By contrast, CSP projects based on parabolic dish systems have not incorporated thermal storage commercially.

A TES system can be ranked by combining performance, cost, and safety criteria. Key performance factors include volumetric energy density or power density, round-trip efficiency, parasitic power use, heat losses, and operating-temperature suitability. Cost and practical metrics cover capital and maintenance cost, footprint or weight, and scalability, while safety and integration depend on material compatibility, environmental impact, and technology maturity or supply availability. A TES with high volumetric energy density can, in theory, store more heat in less space and reduce thermal losses.

TES can be categorized into 3 main types: Sensible heat storage, latent heat storage, and thermochemical storage. Figure XX show that thermochemical storage has highest volumetric energy density followed by latent heat storage and sensible storage.

Sensible Heat System

Sensible heat storage involves storing energy by changing the temperature of a material. The amount of heat that can be stored depends on the material’s mass, specific heat capacity, and the temperature range it experiences as Q = m C p Δ T . The general requirements for sensible heat storage materials used can be summarized as follows:

  • High energy density, achieved through materials with high density and specific heat capacity.
  • Good thermal conductivity (typically greater than 0.3W/mK) and high thermal diffusivity to enable efficient heat transfer.
  • Ease of manufacturing and low cost, making large-scale use practical.
  • Chemical stability, low corrosiveness, and minimal environmental impact, including zero ozone depletion potential (ODP) and low global warming potential (GWP).

A common application of water is the solar water heating tank (illustrated in Fig. XX). In a direct (open-loop system), the solar collector transfers heat directly to the water in the tank, or an indirect (closed-loop) system using a separate HTF that circulates through the collector and a heat exchanger, keeping it isolated from the tank water to prevent contamination and allow the use of antifreeze solutions natural stratification occurs due to changes in density with temperature, hot water rises to the top, cooler water settles at the bottom, and the layer in between forms the thermocline. Another cycle can be used to circulate hot water output and cold water input for supply. Natural thermal stratification enhances the efficiency of TES systems by maintaining temperature layers, which minimizes mixing losses and allows hot water to be drawn at higher temperatures while maximizing overall heat storage capacity.

Thermochemical energy storage

Thermochemical energy storage systems retain heat through reversible chemical reactions or sorption processes. A general reaction can be expressed as:

Heat + C ⇌ A + B ; Q = ΔHreaction

In the charging (endothermic) phase, C splits into B and A, absorbing heat Q. Thermochemical systems store energy in chemical bonds, and this energy remains stable as long as B and A stay separate. When recombined, the reverse (exothermic) reaction reforms A and releases the stored heat.

TCES materials exhibit the highest energy density among thermal storage methods because chemical reactions can store significantly more energy per unit mass or volume.

Although TCES is able to store the highest volumetric energy density, TCES system is not suitable for a small-scale CSP application operating at low temperature because its reaction-based mechanisms require complex reactor designs, and precise control of gas-solid or solid-solid equilibria. Its low technological maturity, slow reaction kinetics, and high material and capital costs make it impractical for low-temperature solar applications.

Latent Heat Storage

Latent heat storage involves a phase change such as melting, evaporation, or crystallization at a constant temperature. Because of the high enthalpy associated with phase transitions, latent TES stores more energy than sensible heat systems of the same size, though it operates over a narrow temperature range. The stored energy is given by Qfusion = mL. Solar thermal energy can be effectively stored as latent heat through the use of phase change materials (PCMs). These materials provide higher energy storage capacity per unit mass or volume. During the daytime solar cycle, the PCM absorbs heat as it changes phase from solid to liquid, storing thermal energy without significant temperature fluctuation.

////When integrated into a water tank, the presence of PCM greatly increases the total heat storage capacity of the system, as illustrated in Fig. xxx./// PCMs are generally classified into three main categories:

  1. Inorganic PCMs, such as hydrated salt solutions composed of natural salts and water
  2. Bio-based PCMs, derived from natural fatty acids like vegetable oils
  3. Organic PCMs, typically made from petroleum-based byproducts

In latent heat storage systems, energy is stored by changing the physical state of the storage medium rather than simply raising its temperature, making it a highly efficient approach for solar thermal applications.
