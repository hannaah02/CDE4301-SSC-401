Final system design

Introduction

In the previous sections, a prototype rig was proven to be able to maintain 10L of water above 40ºC for 9 hours. Here, we propose further design of the rig for deployment under the hotel case study.

The final proposed system will consist of multiple islands, of which contains multiple rigs connected to one TES. (see below)



Thermal transmission system design

The layout for the island for each island size (number of rigs in an island) is shown below. The layout is designed to minimise pipe length while allowing for modular expansion.

As the system is connected to the water mains, existing components must be modified, primarily, 304 stainless steel pipe is used as it is durable, withstands high temperatures and is compatible with water mains [4][5]. The pipe sizes are compared, between:

  1. 1/8" nominal pipe, smallest common size [6]
  2. 3/4" nominal pipe, commonly used in residential plumbing [7]
  3. 1" nominal pipe, commonly used in residential plumbing [7]

Furthermore, two configurations to connect the receivers to the TES are considered:

  1. Parallel (each receiver connected independently to the TES)
  2. Series (receiver connected sequentially)

Finally, the optimal system size for the island has to be determined.

The following assumptions are applied in the calculations within this section:

  • temperature difference across the inlet and outlet pipe of the TES to be 5ºC, which is within the typical range of water thermal systems, [C1][C2] to balance residence time of the fluid within the TES and effectiveness of heat transfer
  • no heat loss through pipe for flow rate calculation, allowing immediate usage of heat energy calculation (Q = mc∆T)
  • turbulent flow in pipes for system head
  • heat loss calculations only considers heat loss through pipes
  • 60ºC water temperature and 25ºC ambient temperature to assess heat loss under high temperature difference situations

Pipe size

For optimal pipe size, the system head is compared across different pipe diameters. A higher system head indicates greater flow resistance, which requires a more powerful pump.

3/4” and 1” pipes provide similar low system head, with minimal differences among the 3 sizes across both configurations. 3/4” pipe is selected for providing comparable hydraulic performance with less material requirements.

Parallel vs series configuration

The thermal efficiency of the piping network is evaluated using the commonly available 25mm thickness aircon insulation that exhibits heat loss of 7.1722W/m, satisfying design requirements of ≤7.95W/m. [8]

The results shows that thermal efficiency decreases with system size in the parallel configuration but increases in the series configuration. This is due to the average pipe length per rig decreasing in the series configuration, but increasing in the parallel configuration. Therefore, the series configuration is chosen for lower heat loss.

Optimal system size

Design 1: Maximum islands

Design 1 focusses on maximising total useful energy collected and the maximum number of islands that fits on one roof is assessed for all island size.

The pipe loss in the entire system indicates island size of ≥3 meets design requirements of 17.5%.

The total useful energy is compared since not all configuration uses all the available space. The total useful energy peaks at system sizes 4 and 6, with system size 6 producing 730.5W less than system size 4 (a 0.9016% reduction). Both configurations have a compact rectangular/square layout that supports modularity and efficient space utilisation. A cost analysis is used to finalise the optimal size.

In design 2, the focus is on meeting the maximum daily hot water requirements of the hotel (7500L). This minimises the energy wastage caused by excessive hot water production (up to 12,880L in design 1) and reduces the overall implementation costs.

The pipe losses in this design indicates island size ≥3 meets design specifications.

Considering the thermal efficiency of the entire system (used to maximise the stored energy), the efficiency peaks at island size 9 with local peak at 6. This corresponds to the trends seen in the total useful energy.

Cost analysis

The 2 island sizes are compared by cost analysis.

Per rig, island size 6 is cheaper by SGD$14.34 (31.41%) and overall 2 roof system for island size 6 is cheaper by SGD$10,767.88 (30.81%). The difference is largely due to the pumps, as the island size of 9 requires more expensive pumps to overcome the higher system head requirements. Overall, the island size 6 is best using the design focussed on meeting hotel requirements, making the cost of the thermal transmission section SGD$24,180.80.

Overall, the final thermal transmission system has a heat loss of 7.1722W/m, pipe loss of 11.6511% and power consumption of 10W per rig, [13] satisfying the design specifications.



Appendix A: System design detailed process with all results

Key assumptions

For flow rate:

  • Temperature difference across TES = 5°C
  • No heat loss in pipe

For heat loss:

  • Ambient temperature difference = 35°C (60ºC water and 25ºC ambient)
  • Heat losses are assumed to occur only through the piping network
  • Assuming turbulent flow in pipe

Pipe size

The pipe sizes to be compared are based on commonly used stainless steel pipe sizes in residential plumbing as well as the smallest size (which is the closest to current hose size on our prototype) commonly available. Prior to the calculation of the head, the flow rate needs to be obtained. The effectiveness of heat transfer depends on the rate of fluid circulation, where a higher flow rate allows more heated fluid to be transported to the TES per unit time. However, very high flow rates reduces the residence time of the fluid within the TES, limiting heat transfer to the PCM. The flow rate is determined using an energy balance across the TES, based on Q = mdot c ∆T. A temperature difference of 5°C is selected, which is within the typical range used in water-based thermal systems. [C1][C2] This provides a balance between achieving sufficient heat transfer while avoiding excessively high flow rates. For the parallel configuration, the flow rate is constant for all the different island sizes (no of rigs) at 0.409 l/min. However, for the series system, the flow rate differs. For the series configuration, the flow rate is determined using an energy balance across the TES, assuming a 5°C temperature rise and neglecting pipe heat loss. The result is below.

The system head is calculated as the sum of frictional losses in the pipe, receiver coil, and fittings:

System head = losses along pipe + minor losses

Pipe losses are calculated using the Darcy–Weisbach equation, while minor losses are estimated using loss coefficients (K-values) for fittings. A constant friction factor of 0.02 is assumed, corresponding to turbulent flow in stainless steel pipes, and tank pressure is assumed based on typical residential water mains pressure.
